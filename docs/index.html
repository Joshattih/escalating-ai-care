The rules baseline

Three tables, one chain. A rule is a line in an official document that tells the clinician what to look for, how to classify it, or what to do (773; in the repository). A state is a rule that says something about the patient at a moment (259). A statement is a state after cleaning, and it is what the raters rate (257): every statement describes one patient at one moment, in the source's own window, with the source's verdict removed, so that any disagreement recorded is a disagreement between roles about a patient and not about what a sentence meant. Each statement keeps its row id, document and page, and the people its guideline says it applies to (the "Applies to" column) and the place its guideline was written for (the "Setting" column). Both are columns of statements.csv.

How the rounds work

Click a stage in the picture, or use the buttons, to light it up and read what happens there. The numbers are real: they are the 75 answers to one statement, a patient aged 12 to 15 who drank alcohol on 6 or more days in the past year.

Guidelines

Fourteen official guidelines, 773 rules, published 2007 to 2026. They cover suicide and self-harm screening, alcohol and drug use in adolescents, crisis lines, and recent advisories on AI in mental health care. Each gives a clinician a line to act on. None was written for an AI tool, and none records where its own authors disagreed.

Statements

Guidelines become statements. We distilled the 773 rules into 257 statements. Each describes one patient at one moment, with the verdict the guideline gave removed. Example: a guideline says a 15-year-old who drinks about twice a month is at highest risk. Kept: the age, how often, the window. Removed: the label. The statement reads: the patient is aged 12 to 15 and drank alcohol on 6 or more days in the past year. The statements are the table at the top of this page.

Roles

Roles give the models a perspective. We wrote 25 clinical roles, three lines each: who you are, who you see, and what you weigh first when deciding to hand a patient over. A model is never asked to be a clinician. It is asked what the decision looks like from that role's perspective. Three models take every role, so every statement is rated by 75 raters.

The prompt

Three parts, fixed before the run. The role (three lines), the statement, and the question with its scale. Only the role lines and the statement change from call to call; the question and the scale are the same for everyone. One call is one model, one role, one statement, and it remembers nothing from any other call.

Round 0

Round 0 is private. Every rater answers the same question, must the screening tool stop and hand this patient to a person now, on a scale from 1 (must keep talking) to 6 (must hand over now), with one sentence of reason. Nobody sees anybody else. For the example statement the 75 answers were: two 2s, sixteen 3s, twenty-eight 4s, fourteen 5s, fifteen 6s. Nobody said I don't know.

Median and package

The group position is the median, not the mean. Sort the 75 answers and take the middle one; it is always an answer somebody gave. When the count is even and the two middle answers differ, both are kept. Here the median is 4. Then a package of reasons is drawn by position: one from a rater at the median, one from the rater farthest above it, one from the rater farthest below, and one from a rater who said I don't know. A slot stays empty when nobody fits; when several fit, one is drawn at random with a fixed seed. Here: a 4 (child psychiatrist, Claude), a 6 (Gemini), a 2 (emergency physician, GPT), no IDK.

Rounds 1 and 2

Two feedback rounds, then stop. Before round 1, every rater is shown its own last answer, the median and the package, minus any reason written by its own model family, and rates again. The emergency physician under GPT had said 2; it was shown the 4 and the 6, not its own 2, and moved to 4. After round 1: sixty-five 4s, nine 5s, one 6. Round 2 repeats this with a package built from the round 1 answers: fifty-four 4s, fourteen 5s, seven 6s. The limit of two rounds follows the RAND/UCLA appropriateness method.

What comes out

For every statement and every round: the median, the spread over the scale, the count of I don't know, who moved and by how much, who was still away from the median after round 2, and every reason with a record of who read it. The heatmap of 257 statements by 75 raters is the picture of the whole run.

The whole pipeline

All of it at once. Guidelines become statements; roles give three models a perspective; a private round, then a median and a package of reasons, then two feedback rounds; out come the medians, the spreads, who moved, and who stayed apart. Models in this run: gpt-4.1-mini, claude-haiku-4-5, gemini-2.5-flash-lite. The next run will ask each role to agree or disagree, on a standard five-point scale, that the tool must hand the patient over.
